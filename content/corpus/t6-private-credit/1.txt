Investor alert: securities-backed lines of credit. Before you pledge your securities as collateral for a loan, understand how the arrangement works and how it can hurt you.

A securities-backed line of credit — SBLOC for short — lets you borrow cash from a lender while you pledge your investment portfolio as collateral. You keep your securities; the lender holds a claim against them. Borrowers use the money for almost anything: a house renovation, tuition, a business stake. What you may not do is use the proceeds to buy more securities — that would turn the arrangement into a margin loan, which is regulated differently.

The lender sets a lending value on your portfolio: a percentage of each asset's market value. High-grade bonds and blue-chip stocks carry a high lending value; volatile assets carry a haircut — the lender discounts them heavily or refuses them outright. A portfolio worth $1 million might support $500,000 to $900,000 of credit depending on its mix. The haircut is the gap between market value and what the lender will actually advance against it.

The risk that the alert keeps returning to is the margin call. If your securities fall in value, the lender demands more cash or more collateral to restore the lending value. Fail to post it and the lender can sell some or all of your securities — at depressed prices, in a falling market, without consulting you. That forced sale locks in losses and can trigger a tax bill at exactly the wrong moment.

The strategy's most aggressive variant has a name in wealth-management circles: buy, borrow, die. Buy appreciating assets and never sell them — the untaxed gains keep compounding. Borrow against the portfolio to fund your lifestyle, since loan proceeds are not income. At death, the unrealized gains pass to heirs with their tax basis stepped up, and the capital gains tax bill the family deferred for decades disappears. Advisors pitch it as having your cake and eating it; critics call it a loophole that lets the largest portfolios compound untaxed while their owners spend borrowed money. The strategy depends entirely on low interest rates and rising markets: when rates rise, the cost of carrying the loan rises with them, and a market slump can force the sale the whole plan was designed to avoid.

Leverage works the same way whether you borrow to buy assets or borrow against them: it magnifies both directions. A portfolio that loses 30% on a 2-to-1 leveraged basis has lost 60% of your equity. Diversification of the underlying portfolio matters less than you would think, because in a crash the correlations all move toward one — the bonds you posted as collateral fall alongside the stocks.

The counterparty matters too. The lender can demand repayment on short notice, change the terms, or reprice the spread after the contract is signed. If the lender fails, your pledged securities sit in the bankruptcy estate until the claim is resolved. And the loan is typically marked to market daily: the amount you can borrow, and the cushion above a call, move with every tick.

Regulators wrote the alert after watching investors discover all of this in a downturn. The advice at its core is plain: read the call provisions, stress-test the portfolio against a falling market before you borrow, and treat the line of credit as the risk instrument it is — not as free money that happens to be secured by your future.
