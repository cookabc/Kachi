BHP Group posted better-than-expected annual earnings and unveiled its largest shareholder payout in four years, propelled by record copper prices that firmly established the metal as the miner's primary profit engine ahead of iron ore.
The CEO, who assumed leadership of the world's largest listed mining company last month, highlighted BHP's development pipeline that could lift copper output by up to 40% by 2035, despite an anticipated dip in production over the near term.
Copper, iron, steelmaking coal and potash are foundational to the way the world is developing. That is why we are moving as fast as we can and bringing these commodities to market.
The mining giant reported a 30% increase in full-year underlying attributable profit to $13.20 billion for the 12 months ended June 30, surpassing the consensus estimate of $12.66 billion.
The company declared a final dividend of 99 cents per share, bringing total annual distributions to $1.72 each — the highest in four years.
Shares jumped as much as 4.2% to A$64.79, reaching a two-month high.
Loved the dividend, a big beat on that, said a portfolio manager. Solid overall, and copper doing all the work, he said, noting that BHP was highlighting long-term growth prospects even though final investment decisions remain pending and capital expenditure is set to climb by more than $1 billion next year.
Copper prices have soared to record highs above $14,000 per metric ton this year, fueled by surging electricity demand from AI data center construction and the worldwide transition to cleaner energy, intensifying the competition among miners for premium copper deposits.
Acquiring copper assets costs roughly five times more than developing them internally.
The red metal, including byproducts like gold and uranium, delivered $18.19 billion in operating earnings for the year, outpacing iron ore's $14.53 billion to become the leading profit contributor.
The company projects copper demand will climb to over 50 million metric tons annually by 2050, up from 34 million tons this year.
The Western Australian iron ore division is contending with labor unrest, though the company anticipates no fallout from the first significant strikes at Port Hedland in decades.
The flagship business generated $14.67 billion in operating earnings, a 2% year-over-year improvement and broadly in line with the consensus.
BHP noted it still has $3.5 billion available to unlock through active portfolio and asset management, part of a broader $10 billion opportunity previously identified. A fund injected $2 billion for a minority interest in the operation's inland power network.
Net debt at the end of the financial year stood at $8.69 billion, coming in below both the company's target range and the projection.
The CEO dismissed speculation that BHP might consider divesting its Queensland metallurgical coal operations over the next one to five years, saying the assets would remain integral to the portfolio if markets evolve as the company anticipates.
A Canadian uranium producer has been exchanging information and holding regular discussions with BHP regarding its uranium project.
