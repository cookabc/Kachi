Indian government bonds weakened on Wednesday, pushing the benchmark yield close to its highest level since March 2023, as high oil prices stoked inflation worries and traders awaited minutes from the central bank's latest policy meeting.
The benchmark 6.94% 2036 bond settled at 7.4427%, compared with 7.3920% on Monday. Bond yields and prices move in opposite directions.
The security carries a coupon of 6.94% and matures in 2036; its principal is repaid at maturity, while the coupon is paid semi-annually.
Brent crude held above $110 a barrel after nearing $112 on Tuesday, its highest in five months, as traders assessed risks to Red Sea shipping and Saudi oil infrastructure.
India imports almost 90% of its crude and is particularly vulnerable to supply disruptions. Sustained high energy prices could lift inflation, pressure the rupee and strain government finances.
September consumer inflation quickened to 5.54%, and economists expect it to exceed the upper end of the central bank's tolerance band in October.
The central bank raised the policy rate and changed its stance earlier this month. Investors will scrutinize minutes of that meeting for signals on how the central bank views oil-driven inflation.
The sovereign yield curve has climbed sharply because of the rate increase and its surprise shift to a calibrated tightening stance, along with high inflation readings, liquidity tightness caused by recent FX measures and elevated oil prices. Analysts expect the 10-year yield to trade between 7.25% and 7.50% in the near term.
Swap markets are pricing in about 100 basis points of rate increases over the next 12 months.
India's overnight indexed swap rates rose in line with oil prices and Treasury yields. The 1-year OIS ended 6 bps higher at 6.59%, the 2-year rate added 5 bps to 6.66%, and the liquid 5-year rate settled at 6.84%.
The government's borrowing programme relies on regular auctions, and dealers watch the bid-to-cover ratio to gauge demand. A weak bid-to-cover forces the central bank to accept a higher cut-off yield.
Primary dealers are required to bid at every auction and absorb unsold supply, which makes their balance-sheet capacity a constraint when issuance is heavy.
Duration measures how much a bond's price moves when yields change; longer maturities carry more duration risk. A portfolio with an average duration of seven years loses roughly 7% for every one percentage point rise in yields.
Foreign holdings of onshore bonds remained unchanged at end-September, and the sovereign's credit rating was left unchanged by the agencies, with the outlook stable. A downgrade would raise refinancing costs for the corporate sector too.
The corporate bond market priced a large fixed-rate issue at a spread of 180 basis points over the comparable government security. The spread widened through the quarter as investors demanded more compensation for credit risk.
Investment grade issuers still found buyers, but a junk bond sale was pulled after investors asked for a spread well beyond the issuer's target. Credit spreads for lower-rated names have widened sharply, while high-grade spreads tightened.
The term premium — the extra yield investors demand for holding a long bond rather than rolling short ones — has risen as supply grows. Parts of the curve are now inverted, with shorter maturities yielding more than longer ones, a pattern that has historically preceded slowdowns.
Inflation-linked bonds, whose principal is adjusted for the consumer price index, drew strong demand. Floating-rate notes also attracted buyers expecting the policy rate to keep rising, while fixed-rate issuance slowed.
Covenants on the new issues restrict additional borrowing, and several issuers have begun refinancing existing debt to push out maturities.

The spread widening was most visible at the long end, where supply has grown fastest. Spread tightening in high-grade names contrasted with the widening in lower-rated credit.
Curve inversion has deepened: the two-year now yields more than the ten-year. Investors read the inversion as a warning about growth rather than as a technical quirk.
Default risk for the weaker issuers is now priced explicitly, with the market demanding a premium over the sovereign for the first time in two years.

Floating-rate notes are quoted at a margin over a benchmark rate, so when the benchmark rate rises the coupon resets higher at the next fixing date.
