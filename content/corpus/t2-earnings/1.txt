BP posted a second-quarter profit that more than doubled to $5.73 billion, surpassing forecasts on the back of stronger energy prices, trading gains, and refining margins, while the chief executive laid out the company's strategic focus.
Oil companies have reaped the rewards this year from market turbulence tied to the U.S.-Iran conflict, which has interrupted energy supply routes and squeezed global availability. Shell last week also reported its quarterly profit doubled to $9.8 billion.
BP's underlying replacement cost profit—its preferred measure of net income—came in above the $5.11 billion consensus from a company-arranged analyst survey and climbed from $2.35 billion in the same period last year. The firm announced a 4% dividend increase to 8.66 cents per ordinary share for the second quarter.
BP additionally said it has initiated procedures to offload its U.S. biogas arm, Archaea, as it scales back clean-energy investments to double down on oil and gas. The company acquired Archaea in 2022 for $4.1 billion as part of an aggressive push into renewables—a direction it scrapped in 2025. Over recent months, BP has written down more than $4 billion, largely tied to Archaea, its solar business, and other low-carbon projects.
In the past few weeks, BP has also finalized the sale of its Gelsenkirchen refinery, reached a deal to offload its Austrian retail operations and declared its plan to sell its UK North Sea assets. By year-end, BP anticipates hitting $15 billion to $16 billion of the $20 billion divestment program it unveiled in 2025, with completion targeted by the close of 2027. The composition of the balance sheet mattered more than hitting the $20 billion figure.
BP's shares, up 25% so far this year, slipped nearly 2% by midday, mirroring weaker oil prices amid hopes for a U.S.-Iran agreement.
The new chief executive set out five priorities: reinforcing the balance sheet, streamlining the portfolio, enforcing stricter investment discipline, enhancing operational efficiency, and building frameworks for faster decision-making and clearer accountability.
We are not making the most of our potential, the chief executive said. Our performance over the past few years has not met our own expectations, let alone those of our shareholders. We have not delivered consistently; we have written off too much value; and our costs and liabilities are not resilient enough in a low price environment.
Analysts noted that acknowledging past shortcomings is a positive move for the investment narrative, and they await greater detail on the financial strategy and concrete next steps.
Though BP is on track to reach its net debt target of $14 billion to $18 billion sooner than originally projected by the end of this year, total liabilities, expected near $40 billion at that point, remained excessive.
BP projects 2026 capital expenditure of $13.5 billion to $14 billion, reflecting a choice to postpone selling stakes in U.S. offshore assets. Earlier guidance stood at $13 billion to $13.5 billion.
BP achieved its largest quarterly net profit since 2022, with all divisions beating expectations in the second quarter. Pre-tax earnings at its customers and products unit, housing BP's substantial oil trading operation, hit $4.95 billion—above the $4.46 billion average forecast in an analyst poll and versus $1.53 billion a year earlier.
The company reported upstream plant reliability slipped to 92.4% in the second quarter from 95.7% in the prior quarter, while output fell to 2.2 million barrels of oil equivalent per day and refinery throughput declined, partly due to scheduled maintenance and disruptions from the Middle East conflict.
Brent crude averaged about $97 per barrel in the second quarter, climbing from $78 in the first quarter and $67 a year earlier.
