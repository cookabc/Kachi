MMG offered to guarantee ferronickel supplies to European stainless steel producers, aiming to secure EU antitrust approval for its $500 million bid for a rival's Brazilian nickel business.
The offer followed a combative closed-door hearing during which the mining company and the seller defended the transaction before European Commission officials and third parties.
The Commission has warned that MMG could divert supplies from Europe to China, potentially damaging the bloc's producers. A state-owned group owns 67% of MMG, while institutional investors hold the rest.
An executive said the offer is a legally enforceable guarantee to maintain existing supply levels and can be independently monitored.
Under the commitment, customers that are already the target's customers would be able to obtain the same volume they currently buy for the lifetime of the mine.
The seller has threatened to close the mine if the EU watchdog blocks the deal. It said the remedy should lead the Commission to approve the transaction, and a spokesperson called it a supply security insurance policy that removes the competition concerns raised.
The Commission extended its deadline for a decision on the deal to December 21.

Separately, a mid-cap producer reported quarterly results that required investors to look past several one-off items. The company booked an impairment charge against a gas field whose reserves were revised lower, and a smaller write-down on inventory. A restructuring programme announced with the results will cut overhead, at the cost of a one-off charge this year.
On an adjusted basis, ignoring those items, earnings per share came in slightly above the consensus estimate, though reported net income was flat. Management said payout would be maintained, and the payout ratio would stay within its stated range.
The shares trade at a discount to peers on a p/e basis but at a premium on p/b, which analysts said reflects a book value inflated by past capital spending. Book value per share has barely moved in three years while the market cap has halved, leaving the stock below one times book.
A share issuance last year funded the acquisition, and the company has not bought back stock since. Several brokerages cut their target price and trimmed forecasts for the year, citing headwinds from freight costs that outweighed tailwinds from a weaker currency.
Operating expenditure per unit fell, but capital expenditure guidance was raised, and the company said it would review its portfolio for possible divestitures. A spin-off of the logistics unit remains under consideration, alongside a possible listing of the unit on a separate exchange.
A shareholder letter criticised the board for pursuing a merger of equals that would have diluted existing holders, and urged management to focus on free cash flow and net debt reduction instead.
For the sector, the quarter's pattern was consistent: reported figures flattered by one-off gains, adjusted figures that missed, and valuations that still assume the cycle turns. Analysts noted the market cap of the smaller players now sits below replacement cost.

The company's EPS on an adjusted basis beat the consensus estimate by three cents, while reported EPS was flat year on year. Opex per unit fell 8%, and management said opex discipline would continue into next year.
A person close to the process said a stakeholder group including the works council had been briefed before the announcement. An IPO of the logistics arm remains an alternative to the spin-off, and a listing would give existing holders shares in the separated business.
