We backtest a simple momentum overlay on a universe of the ten largest energy futures, rebalancing monthly, from 2010 to 2024. The strategy is long the contracts in the top half by twelve-month return and flat otherwise.
In-sample, from 2010 to 2019, the strategy produces a Sharpe ratio of 1.4 and a maximum drawdown of 18%. The hit rate is 54%, and the expectancy per trade is 0.6%. Alpha against the benchmark is 4.2% a year, with a beta of 0.35, so most of the return is not explained by market exposure.
Out-of-sample, from 2020 to 2024, the Sharpe falls to 0.4 and the maximum drawdown widens to 31%. The drop is what you expect when a specification is overfitting the first decade. A walk-forward test — refitting each year on data available at the time and trading the next year — produces a Sharpe of 0.6, between the two.
Three biases would inflate these numbers if we were careless. Look-ahead bias appears if a signal uses data published after the decision point, for example a weekly inventory report released on Wednesday used in a Tuesday trade. Survivorship bias appears if the universe is taken from today's list of contracts, because contracts that were delisted are missing and the sample is therefore easier than the one a live trader faced. Data snooping appears if we try many variants and report the best, which is what multiple testing does: at a 5% significance level, twenty independent tests produce roughly one false positive by construction.
Turnover matters. Rebalancing monthly at 12% one-way turnover, with 6 basis points of transaction cost and 3 basis points of slippage per side, costs about 1.3% a year — most of the gross alpha. The result is sensitive to the cost assumption: doubling slippage removes the edge entirely.
Capacity is a separate constraint. The strategy trades the front two contracts in markets where a few hundred lots move the price, so position sizing is capped well below the notional the backtest implies. A capacity constraint this tight means the strategy cannot absorb meaningful capital without slippage rising; the liquidity constraint binds at roughly $40 million.
Correlation across the ten contracts is high, around 0.7, so the diversification the position sizing assumes is partly illusory. Risk is concentrated in a single factor, which a stress test on the 2020 oil crash makes obvious: the overlay loses 14% in that month alone.
We run a scenario analysis on three paths — a repeat of 2020, a slow grind higher, and a range-bound market — and report the sensitivity of the Sharpe to the lookback window. A robustness check across lookback windows from six to eighteen months shows the sign of the edge holding in most but not all; the strategy is not robust to the shortest window.
Value at risk at the 95% level is 2.1% daily, but value at risk understates tail risk here, as it always does when returns are fat-tailed. The earlier drawdown is the more honest measure of what the strategy can do.

Tracking error against the benchmark is 6.5% a year, which is high for a long-only overlay but consistent with the concentrated factor exposure. We report both max drawdown and tracking error because a strategy can look steady against the index and still lose badly in absolute terms.

The rebalance dates are fixed at month-end rather than signalled by the data, so the schedule itself introduces no look-ahead. We also test a quarterly rebalance, which halves turnover and lowers the return by roughly a third.
