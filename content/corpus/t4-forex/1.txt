The euro declined on Friday and was headed for a weekly loss against both the US dollar and the yen, as a selloff in French government bonds drove the country's debt risk premium to its highest since the sovereign crisis.
The yen was set for a weekly gain against the dollar, supported by safe-haven demand after Thursday's selloff in AI-related stocks. The dollar index was almost unchanged over the week.
The euro came under pressure from concerns about rising unrest in France and the country's worsening budget problems. The country also faces the possibility of a rating downgrade.
The euro was down 0.21% at $1.1191 and was on course to end the week 0.43% lower. It fell more sharply against the yen, touching 175.08 yen, its lowest since October 2025.
The dollar index, which measures the greenback against a basket including the yen and the euro, rose 0.25% to 101.82.
The yen had continued to trade at firmer levels late in the week, encouraged by more risk-off trading conditions. When risk appetite fades, funding currencies such as the yen tend to strengthen.
Traders were looking ahead to next week's policy meetings at the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan. All three are expected to leave rates unchanged, but investors will seek clues about the future policy path.
After Thursday's stock market selloff, fed funds futures traders reduced bets on a December rate hike, though they still saw an 81% chance of such a move.
Traders priced a 66% chance of an ECB hike in December. Investors will scrutinize any signal on the rate outlook after the ECB president said a moderate policy response to inflation was appropriate.
Monetary policy is now diverging: the Federal Reserve is holding while the ECB signals patience, and the Bank of Japan is edging toward normalisation. Forward guidance from each central bank matters more than the decisions themselves, because markets trade the expected path rather than today's rate.
The Bank of Japan has been tapering its bond purchases, and a further reduction would tighten global liquidity. Quantitative easing in the past decade pushed yield-seeking capital into emerging markets; tapering reverses part of that flow.
On the fiscal side, France's budget deficit has widened, and fiscal policy is now working against monetary policy rather than with it. A looser fiscal stance while the central bank holds rates tends to widen credit spreads on government debt.
Purchasing Managers' Index data showed private-sector growth in the euro zone slowed in October to its weakest pace in four months, hurt by a renewed contraction in France.
The drop brought the PMIs back in line with other growth indicators rather than suggesting downside risks from here. France remained a drag, but the rest of the euro area was resilient.
In Japan, Friday's data showed annual core consumer inflation exceeded the central bank's 2% target in September for the first time in nine months. The reading pointed to broadening price pressures and strengthened the case for a near-term rate hike.
Economists debated whether the world is heading for deflation as goods prices fall while services stay sticky, or stagflation if growth stalls with inflation still above target. A soft landing — inflation returning to target without a recession — remains the central case, but the probability has narrowed.
In emerging Asia, the rupiah and the peso weakened as the dollar firmed. Indonesia's central bank intervened in the foreign-exchange market to slow the rupiah's slide, and traders estimated the intervention at roughly $500 million.
Countries running a current account deficit depend on capital inflows to fund it, so a stronger dollar tightens their financing conditions quickly. A trade balance that swings into deficit adds to the pressure.
The carry trade — borrowing in a low-yielding currency to invest in a higher-yielding one — has become less attractive as rate differentials narrow. Unwinding carry positions amplifies moves in the funding currencies, which is why the yen can rally sharply when risk appetite turns.
Sterling rose against the dollar and the euro after inflation data reinforced rate hike expectations. The yuan hit its highest level since 2022 on a weaker dollar and resilient exports.
Investors expect the Brazilian real and the peso to fall after the election result, and analysts said intervention alone cannot offset a widening fiscal gap. The currency market is where policy credibility gets marked to market first.

A dovish surprise — a central bank signalling fewer hikes than expected — weakens a currency, while a hawkish one strengthens it. Markets read the tone before they read the numbers.

Capital flows into emerging markets slowed as the dollar firmed, and portfolio inflows turned negative for the quarter.
