The oil market is now telling two very different stories. Brent crude at around $88 a barrel has dropped by nearly a third from the $126 peak seen during the height of the Iran war, and a diplomatic breakthrough or the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz could push it lower still. Yet the prices of the products made from that crude have not fallen nearly as much. The result is a squeeze on consumers and unusually generous profits for refiners.
The reason lies in the gap between how quickly crude supply can recover versus how fast refining capacity can be restored. There is no equivalent bypass for refined fuels. The International Energy Agency estimates that Middle Eastern refinery throughput was 2.9 million barrels per day below pre-war levels in the second quarter and will remain 2.2 million bpd short in the third. Ukrainian strikes have also knocked Russian processing to near a two-decade low, while reduced Chinese runs and fuel exports add to the shortfall.
Diesel is the hardest hit. Combined exports from Russia, the Middle East and key Asian suppliers ran 1.3 million bpd lower in July than a year earlier, roughly one-fifth of the seaborne trade that importers depend on. Europe has little cushion: gasoil inventories at the Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Antwerp hub, a close proxy for diesel, sit 24% below their five-year average, while jet fuel stocks are 39% below that mark.
Refiners are exploiting the tightness. The premium European diesel commands over Brent, a rough proxy for the profit of turning crude into fuel, has nearly tripled from around $25 at the start of the year to more than $70. Jet fuel is a different story, since high prices can discourage discretionary flying. Diesel, however, is harder to ration: trucks, farms and factories all need it.
Investors have taken note. Shares of Valero Energy, Marathon Petroleum and Phillips 66 have climbed an average of 66% since the Iran war began, while ExxonMobil, Chevron, Shell, BP and TotalEnergies have managed only an 8% gain. The divergence is logical: pure refiners are leveraged to processing margins, while oil majors carry more exposure to crude prices that have fallen steeply from their crisis high.
There is some relief on the horizon. New and expanded refineries in Asia, the Middle East and Africa will bring more capacity online. But the long-term outlook was already tight before the current disruptions. Analysts project refined fuel demand will rise by 2.5 million bpd over the next three years, while net capacity adds only 1.2 million bpd. And building a new refinery takes eight to ten years.
A potential Iran peace deal could knock crude prices down further, but it cannot repair damaged refineries or swiftly refill depleted fuel stocks. Cheaper crude, therefore, may take longer to show up at the pump.
