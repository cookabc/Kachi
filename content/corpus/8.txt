The chief executive of the state-owned energy giant has warned that global oil stockpiles are critically low, revealing that more than 2.6 billion barrels of crude have been lost since the military confrontation erupted. That volume, he said, represents nearly a month's worth of worldwide production under normal conditions, highlighting the severity of a crisis that has shut a key strait, rattled energy markets, and shown no clear path to resolution.
Speaking on Tuesday, the chief executive said that even if the strait reopened immediately, replenishing depleted inventories would take up to 18 months at an average pace of 2.1 million barrels per day. The company, the largest oil exporter in the world, reported a 44% jump in second-quarter net profit to $32.69 billion, boosted by higher prices for crude, refined fuels and petrochemicals, while rerouting shipments.
Despite the unprecedented supply disruption, we continued to demonstrate our ability to maintain business continuity by capitalizing on our diverse asset base, he said.
The crisis may widen further. Forces last month declared a blockade on the petroleum sector, adding new strain on shipping and extending the reach of the conflict. That blockade endangers both the cross-country pipeline, which carries crude to the Red Sea as a key alternative route, and export facilities along the coast.
Militants also struck oil infrastructure at two ports last month, opening a second front in the war. The chief executive acknowledged that recent attacks caused some production stoppages but expressed confidence that operations could resume swiftly.
He credited storage capacity, export terminals and the ability to boost flows through the pipeline as essential to staying resilient. Emergency stock releases, commercial inventory drawdowns, demand moderation and the pipeline itself had helped blunt the global impact.
He warned that the upheaval had exposed fragilities in the world's refining network, noting that strong refining margins reflect persistent tightness in fuel markets while plants worldwide run near peak capacity. The industry has limited flexibility to handle additional shocks, and any major unplanned outage could put further strain on supplies.
Asked how fast production could recover, he replied that output could revert to pre-conflict levels within days, and could reach maximum sustainable capacity in three weeks if needed. Total hydrocarbon output averaged 9.5 million barrels per day in the second quarter, down from 12.8 million a year earlier.
The market cap of the company has been volatile through the conflict, and analysts noted that its valuation now embeds a permanent geopolitical discount. On a p/e basis the shares trade below the five-year average, which some read as cheap and others as a warning that earnings are at a cyclical peak.
The producing countries face a widening fiscal deficit if crude stays below their budget breakeven, and several have already cut spending. For importers the arithmetic is reversed: a sustained price above their assumption widens the trade deficit and pressures the currency.
Analysts expect at least one central bank in the region to deliver a rate cut if growth slows, while importers may face the opposite pressure and need to raise rates to defend the exchange rate.

Importers that rely on commodity exports expect their currency to appreciate when prices rise, and to fall when the cycle turns.
